### What Are Greeks and Why Do Traders Use Them (~3 min)
Greeks measure how an option's price changes in response to different market inputs. They're the risk dashboard for options positions. You don't need to know the calculus — you need to know what each number means when you're watching SPY move 1% in a day.

Cover each Greek in trader terms (see Key Concepts below), then show the formula inputs:
- Spot price (S) — from your OHLCV data
- Strike price (K) — from the options chain
- Time to expiration (T) — in years, computed from today and expiry date
- Risk-free rate (r) — use 3-month T-bill rate (hardcoded as a constant, fine for this use case)
- Implied volatility (σ) — from the options chain (Alpaca provides IV)

## The Code

```bash
# Compute Greeks for all options data
python ingestion/greeks_calculator.py \
  --options-dir data/options/ \
  --ohlcv-dir data/ohlcv/ \
  --output-dir data/options_with_greeks/

# Find high-theta ATM options (good for covered call writing)
duckdb -c "
  SELECT
    symbol,
    expiration_date,
    strike_price,
    option_type,
    ROUND((bid + ask) / 2, 2) as mid_price,
    ROUND(delta, 3) as delta,
    ROUND(theta, 3) as theta,
    ROUND(implied_volatility * 100, 1) as iv_pct
  FROM read_parquet('data/options_with_greeks/**/*.parquet')
  WHERE option_type = 'call'
    AND delta BETWEEN 0.25 AND 0.45
    AND theta < -0.05
    AND open_interest > 500
  ORDER BY theta ASC
  LIMIT 20
"
```

## Key Concepts

**Delta — the directional exposure**
Delta measures how much an option's price moves for a $1 move in the underlying. A call with delta 0.5 gains $0.50 if the stock goes up $1. Delta also approximates the probability the option expires in the money: a 0.30 delta call has roughly a 30% chance of finishing in the money. Traders targeting high-probability income trades (covered calls, cash-secured puts) typically look for 0.20–0.35 delta.

**Gamma — the rate of change of delta**
Gamma measures how fast delta changes as the stock moves. High gamma options (typically at-the-money, near expiration) can swing from 0.4 delta to 0.7 delta in a single day. This is why short-dated ATM options are risky to sell — a 2% move can turn a small credit into a large loss fast. As a buyer, high gamma is what you want if you expect a big move.

**Theta — time decay (the options seller's friend)**
Theta measures how much value an option loses per day just from the passage of time — everything else held equal. A theta of -0.05 means the option loses $5 per day (per 100-share contract). Options sellers collect theta; options buyers pay it. Theta accelerates into expiration, especially for ATM options. Selling options with 30–45 DTE (days to expiration) and letting theta decay is one of the most common retail income strategies.

**Vega — sensitivity to volatility**
Vega measures how much the option's price changes for a 1% change in implied volatility. A vega of 0.20 means the option gains $0.20 in value if IV rises 1%. When you buy options before earnings, you're often buying vega — hoping IV expands before the event. When IV collapses after earnings ("IV crush"), option buyers lose on vega even if the stock moves in their direction.

## Common Gotchas

- **"Greeks are NaN for some rows"** — py_vollib requires a positive time-to-expiration. Contracts that expired today (T=0) will produce NaN or errors. The script filters these out.
- **"My delta values don't match what I see on my broker"** — Different brokers use slightly different volatility surfaces and interest rate assumptions. Small differences (0.01–0.03 delta) are normal.
- **"Theta is positive for some puts"** — This can happen for deep in-the-money puts near expiration where early exercise value dominates. It's a known edge case in Black-Scholes for American-style options.
- **"The risk-free rate is hardcoded"** — Yes, we use a constant (e.g., 5.25%). This is fine for the screener use case. If you want dynamic rates, look up the 3-month T-bill yield from FRED or yfinance's `^IRX`.
